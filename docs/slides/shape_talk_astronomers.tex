\begin{frame}{What is in an alert, 1/3: the cutout triplet}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.47\textwidth]{talk_triplet_explained.png}
  \vspace{-0.5em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Science:} tonight's exposure, $63{\times}63$ pixels ($\approx 1'$)
        around the candidate: transient, host, neighbours, sky.
      \item \textbf{Template:} a deep stack of earlier images of the same field.
        The sky \emph{before}.
      \item \textbf{Difference:} the survey pipeline's subtraction. What changed.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    \textbf{Why all three?}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item The difference is \emph{not} science minus template of these crops: the
        pipeline makes it on full frames with PSF matching and resampling the
        crops cannot reproduce.
      \item The template gives host context: nuclear, offset, on a star, empty sky.
      \item Science + template together expose bad subtractions.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What is in an alert, 3/3: the metadata}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}

    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{talk_metadata_annotated.png}
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \footnotesize
    About 30 numbers the pipeline has already computed for every alert, mostly
    about \emph{context}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item nearest catalogued source: distance, brightness, star or galaxy
      \item how point-like the detection is
      \item the survey's own real-vs-bogus score
      \item how many detections so far
    \end{itemize}

    already know about that spot. Also \alert{switched off} in today's toy.

\begin{frame}{The idea: a 256-byte fingerprint per alert}
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      box/.style={draw=vizblue, thick, rounded corners, minimum height=1.4cm,
                  minimum width=2.6cm, align=center, font=\small},
      arr/.style={-{Latex[length=2.5mm]}, thick, vizgrey}]
    \node[box] (a) {alert\\light curve + cutouts};
    \node[box, right=1.1cm of a, fill=vizblue!10] (e) {encoder\\(a trained network)};
    \node[box, right=1.1cm of e] (z) {fingerprint\\64 numbers = 256 bytes};
    \draw[arr] (a) -- (e);
    \draw[arr] (e) -- (z);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \vspace{0.8em}

  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{The analogy you already use}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item A full spectrum $\to$ (colour, magnitude) on a CMD.
      \item Two numbers, chosen by physics, that preserve what matters.
      \item A fingerprint is the same idea with more numbers, chosen by training
        on 18\,000 labelled ZTF transients.
    \end{itemize}
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
    \textbf{What you can do with fingerprints}
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Classify: 87\,\% accuracy from 256 bytes (full pipeline: 94\,\%).
      \item Find look-alikes: nearest neighbours in fingerprint space.
      \item Flag outliers: things far from everything seen before.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why a toy: circles and squares}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{talk_stamps_row.png}
  \vspace{0.6em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Before trusting this on real transients, test the image part alone on a
      problem whose answer we know.
    \item Light-curve input switched off: the fingerprint depends only on the cutout.
    \item Synthetic stamps: circles and squares of equal area, random size, position
      and brightness, plus noise. \emph{Nothing but shape distinguishes them.}
    \item If the fingerprint cannot separate these, nothing downstream is meaningful.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two ways to build the image encoder}
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      box/.style={draw=vizblue, thick, rounded corners, minimum height=2.4cm,
                  text width=5.2cm, align=center, font=\small}]
    \node[box] (a) {\textbf{From scratch}\\[0.3em]
      0.25 million parameters\\ starts knowing nothing\\ learns only from our stamps};
    \node[box, right=1.2cm of a, fill=vizblue!10] (b) {\textbf{Zoobot}\\[0.3em]
      8.5 million parameters\\ pre-trained on Galaxy Zoo morphology votes\\
      weights frozen: used exactly as published};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \vspace{0.8em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Zoobot is a public model (Walmsley et al.). We never retrain its weights;
      we only train a small read-out that maps its output into our 16 numbers.
    \item A first-year student vs.\ someone who has classified galaxies for a decade.
      Both get the same homework.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The discovery test: shapes it has never seen}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{talk_novel_row.png}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Train on circles and squares only. Then show four new shapes and ask,
      \emph{without telling it}:
    \begin{enumerate}
      \item Is this fingerprint far from every circle and square we trained on?
        (score: how often a new shape looks more unusual than a known one;
        50\,\% useless, 100\,\% perfect)
      \item If we group the fingerprints blindly, do the new shapes get their own groups?
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Ceiling check: \emph{if} we had labels, could a straight line separate all six?
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The catch: teaching it to classify squashes the fingerprint}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=0.6\textheight]{talk_pca_pair.png}
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
    \small
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Once the read-out learns circle vs.\ square, the fingerprint collapses
        onto a line.
      \item Six-shape separability: 99.5\,\% $\to$ 85\,\%. Ring now sits on top of
        circle.
      \item New-shape detection holds up (96\,\%): each shape keeps its own stretch
        of the line.
      \item \alert{Classification rewards one direction and lets the others fade.}
        Adding ``reconstruct the image'' recovers most of it (97\,\%).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What this means, and what is next}
  \textbf{Three takeaways}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Use a Galaxy Zoo pre-trained encoder for cutouts. From scratch is not viable
      for rare classes.
    \item Be deliberate about how hard the fingerprint is pushed to classify; keep a
      reconstruction-type objective in the mix.
    \item Measure discoverability on real alerts next: train without TDEs, then ask
      whether TDEs look unusual.
  \end{enumerate}
  \textbf{Honest caveats}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Synthetic shapes are easy. Real stamps have hosts, PSF blur and faint sources.
    \item Not yet tested: whether an ordinary ImageNet-pre-trained network does as well.
      If it does, the win is ``pre-training'', not ``Galaxy Zoo''.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: how the numbers are defined}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Separability (with labels):} 6-way logistic regression on the 16-number
      fingerprint, 5-fold cross-validated accuracy.
    \item \textbf{New-shape detection:} mean distance to the 5 nearest circle/square
      training fingerprints; AUROC of new vs.\ known.
    \item \textbf{Blind grouping:} $k$-means with $k{=}6$; adjusted mutual information
      against the true shape.
    \item \textbf{Dims used:} participation ratio of the fingerprint covariance
      (16 = all directions equal, 1 = a line).
    \item \textbf{The 2-D maps:} PCA shows the two largest directions (honest, can hide
      structure); t-SNE folds all 16 into the plane (shows structure, exaggerates gaps).
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
